BONZA · AI PRODUCT · 2025

An AI meal plan that proposes, explains, and waits for a yes

ROLE Product Designer: strategy, AI behaviour, UX, UI, developer handoff
TIMELINE 3 months
TEAM Designers, developers,
a manager and QA
SKILLS UX for AI
Product Strategy
Systems Thinking
OUTCOME

In staging: a plan that keeps adjusting after checkout, with a success plan ready for launch. No live data yet.

CONSTRAINT

Built on WooCommerce: every AI change must map to a product variation swap or a renewal interval change.

KEY DECISION

The AI never changes a paid order on its own. It proposes, explains why, and the owner confirms.

TRADE-OFF

One question every 2 weeks instead of a weekly survey: less data, but owners actually answer.

OVERVIEW

Every dog food subscription is smart at signup, then frozen. I designed one that keeps learning, without taking control away from the owner.

Bonza is a dog meal-plan subscription. Like every competitor, it used a short quiz and a fixed formula to set a portion and recipe once. But dogs change: they gain weight, slow down, get older, go off their food. The plan didn't.

I redesigned the experience so the quiz is only the starting point. After checkout, an AI layer reads small signals the owner can give in two seconds (weight, leftovers, energy, stool quality) and suggests adjustments the way a good vet nutritionist would. The hard part wasn't the AI. It was designing when the AI is allowed to act, how it explains itself, and what happens when it isn't sure.

PROBLEM

The category stops paying attention the moment you pay.

A competitive review of tails.com, Butternut Box and Different Dog showed the same gap everywhere: plans only change when the owner notices a problem and goes into settings to fix it.

WHAT HAPPENS AFTER SIGNUP
Competitors: owner has to notice and act
Some recipe reasoning shown at signup
Bonza + AI: proactive check-ins every ~2 weeks

The plan goes stale

Age, weight, activity and health change. The portion doesn't, unless the owner remembers to edit it.

Nobody knows if it's working

Leftover food or a dog going off a recipe isn't captured anywhere. The subscription keeps shipping the same thing.

Fixing it feels like starting over

Redoing a 5-step quiz feels like admitting the first answer was wrong. Cancelling is easier.

PRINCIPLES

Three rules every AI behaviour had to follow.

Before designing screens, I wrote the rules the AI would be held to. They settled most later debates, because any idea that broke a rule was out.

1. Propose, never impose

The owner is paying for every delivery. The AI can suggest any change, but nothing in a paid order changes without a yes.

2. Always show the why

Every suggestion comes with one plain-language reason tied to what the owner told us: "Bella left food twice this month, so we suggest a smaller portion."

3. Know its limits

The AI adjusts food, not health. Anything that looks medical goes to a vet, using the "worth speaking to your vet" framing Bonza already trusts.

TRUST MODEL

What the AI does alone, what it asks about, and what it never touches.

Deciding the AI's level of autonomy was the core design decision. I split every possible action into three levels, based on the cost of the AI being wrong.

Does on its own

Personalizes copy with the dog's name, writes the plain-language reasoning on the result page, schedules the next check-in. Low risk, easy to ignore.

Proposes, owner confirms

Portion size, recipe mix, bag size and delivery frequency. These change what the owner pays for and what the dog eats, so they always need a yes.

Never does

Makes medical claims, diagnoses conditions, or changes a paid subscription automatically. Health concerns are sent to a vet instead.

ONBOARDING

A 5-step quiz that gives the AI a starting point, not a final answer.

I kept Bonza's familiar 5-step structure so existing owners wouldn't have to relearn it. What changed is what happens with the answers: they feed an AI model instead of a fixed lookup table, and every step has a clear job.

1. About your dog

Name, sex and basic profile. No calculation yet, but it personalizes every screen and message that follows ("Bella's daily portion", not "your dog's daily portion"), which makes the AI feel attentive rather than generic.

2. Breed and age

Sets the calorie baseline. Age changes how many calories a dog burns more than most owners expect, so this step quietly does the heaviest lifting.

3. Size, shape, and activity

Weight, body condition in everyday words ("a little thin", "ideal", "carrying a bit extra") and daily exercise. Plain language gets more honest answers than asking owners to score their dog.

4. Health

Condition tags like joints, digestion, weight management and anxiety. They steer which ingredients the recipe prioritizes but never the calorie number, and higher-stakes conditions trigger a "worth speaking to your vet" note. This is principle 3 in action.

5. Current food

What the dog eats now and how much of the diet Bonza should cover (25/50/75/100%). Used for transition planning and bag size, not the core calculation.

Result page: the first proof the AI can explain itself

The model generates the starting portion, recipe mix and bag size, and writes the reason for each in plain language for that specific dog. Bonza's existing "what's in there for Bella" copy was already close to this pattern; the AI makes it specific instead of templated. This is where owners first learn that the plan will explain itself, which sets up trust for every suggestion after checkout.

AFTER CHECKOUT

The adjustment loop: small signals in, clear proposals out, owner decides.

This is the part no competitor has. Every two weeks, the owner gets one conversational question. The AI combines the answers over time and only speaks up when there's enough evidence to suggest a change.

ONE CYCLE
1. One-question check-in: "Did Bella finish her bowl today?"
2. AI looks at the pattern, not a single answer
3. Proposal with one plain reason
4. Owner accepts, edits or declines
5. Change applies from the next delivery

Designing for the states beyond the happy path

An AI feature is only as trustworthy as its worst moment, so I designed what happens when things don't go to plan:

The owner doesn't reply

Nothing changes. Silence is never treated as a yes. The plan keeps shipping as it is, and the next check-in arrives as normal.

The AI isn't sure

With too few or mixed signals, the AI asks one follow-up question instead of guessing. It never proposes a change it can't explain.

Something looks medical

If answers suggest a health problem rather than a food problem, the AI doesn't adjust food. It suggests speaking to a vet, and the plan stays as it is.

The owner says no

Declining is one tap and needs no explanation. The plan stays unchanged and the AI doesn't repeat the same suggestion next cycle.

DECISIONS & TRADE-OFFS

Three calls that shaped the product, and what I gave up for each.

1. One question every 2 weeks, not a weekly survey

Considered: a short weekly in-app survey, as thorough as the onboarding quiz. More data, better adjustments on paper.
Chose: a single push-style question every two weeks, worded like a person would ask it ("Did Bella finish her bowl today?", not "Rate food consumption 1–5").
Why: people subscribe to a meal plan so they can stop thinking about it. A weekly survey turns it into a chore, and an AI with no answers learns nothing. I traded data density for response rate.

2. Owner confirms every change, instead of auto-adjusting

Considered: letting the AI apply small portion changes automatically, which would feel more magical.
Chose: every change to a paid order is a proposal the owner accepts, edits or declines.
Why: one wrong automatic change to food or billing could break trust in the whole feature. A confirmation step costs one tap; losing trust costs the subscription.

3. Design inside WooCommerce, not around it

Considered: a custom adjustment engine with freeform recommendations.
Chose: every AI proposal maps to something WooCommerce Subscriptions already supports: swapping a product variation (recipe and bag size) or changing the renewal interval. Account pages reuse the native pause, skip and cancel patterns owners already know.
Why: it could ship without a custom backend, and the AI feels like part of the store rather than a bolted-on app. The cost: the AI can only suggest changes the store can actually sell.

MEASURING SUCCESS

In staging now. Here's how I'll know if it works.

Bonza is in staging. I'm checking the five quiz steps, the result page and the subscription screens against their expected states across common screen sizes before launch. There's no live data yet, so instead of guessing at results, I defined what success looks like before launch:

Do owners answer?

Check-in response rate. If people don't reply, the AI can't learn. This tests the "one question" bet.

Do they trust the suggestions?

Proposal acceptance rate, plus how often owners edit before accepting. Low acceptance means the reasoning isn't convincing.

Does it keep them?

Retention and cancellations compared with the old fixed plan. The business case is that a plan that adapts is a plan people keep.

This section will be updated with real numbers after launch.

REFLECTION

What this project taught me about designing AI.

Autonomy is a design decision, not a model setting.

The most important work wasn't the quiz or the screens. It was deciding, action by action, how much the AI is allowed to do on its own based on the cost of being wrong.

The AI is only as good as a two-second answer.

A smart adjustment model is worthless if owners don't respond. The real problem was behavioural, not computational.

A good calculator isn't a relationship.

Bonza already got the first calculation right. The value is in the months after, which almost nobody in this category designs for.

What I'd do next

Test the wording of proposals with real owners after launch, and use acceptance and decline reasons to tune when the AI speaks up and how it explains itself.
