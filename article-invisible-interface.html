The best interface is the step you remove

By Vita Addelia, Product Designer

In 2023 I was fascinated by "invisible interfaces": voice, gestures and screens that disappear. Today I think about it more plainly. The most valuable invisible interface is a step the user no longer has to take. And the more you hide, the more carefully you must show control when it matters.

Invisible effort: the system does the work

My QA automation work is a good example of design users never see. I built 413 automated tests across more than 200 pages, run with one API call after every deployment. They check that the cart opens, the mobile menu slides in and the close button works. No visitor will ever see a test, but every visitor gets a site where those things work.

Invisible doesn't mean uncontrolled

Bonza is an AI dog meal-plan subscription. The plan keeps adjusting using signals owners already have easy access to: weight check-ins, leftover food, stool quality and energy level. That part is quiet by design. But the AI never changes a paid subscription on its own. It proposes every adjustment, and the owner confirms it.

Make the effort invisible. Keep the control visible.

Anything involving money, health or something that cannot be undone needs a visible moment of consent. Hide that, and "invisible" quickly becomes "untrustworthy".

Hide complexity, not information

Doctors at NOTES Healthcare ERP did not want a spreadsheet of deductions. One said it plainly: "Is everything okay this month? Yes or no." So the salary dashboard leads with the answer and keeps the detail one tap away, where every number is traceable. The complexity is still there; people just do not have to wade through it.

Good structure is invisible when it works

In the Catalog Heist, a 500,000-product store, one of the first findings was that categories should behave like filters, not like steps people must click through. Nobody notices information architecture when it is right. They just find the product.

A checklist for invisible design

  • Which step can the system do for the user, safely?
  • If the system acts on its own, can the user see what it did and undo it?
  • Does anything involving money, health or irreversible change require a clear confirmation?
  • Is the detail still reachable for the people who need it?

This is a rewrite of my 2023 Medium article "Did you know that we could design an invisible interface?" (March 2023), updated with what I have learned since and with examples from real projects.
