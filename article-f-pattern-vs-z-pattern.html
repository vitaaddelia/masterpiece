F-pattern vs. Z-pattern, revisited: design for how people actually scan

By Vita Addelia, Product Designer

In 2023 I wrote a short article comparing the F-pattern and the Z-pattern, with tips for using each. Three years and a lot of shipped screens later, I would write it differently. The patterns are not wrong, but I treated them as layouts to copy. I now use them as a way to diagnose a screen.

What I got wrong in 2023

  • The F-pattern is not a goal. In Nielsen Norman Group's eye-tracking research, the F-pattern shows up when people face text without strong formatting: they read the first lines, then scan down the left edge and skip most of the rest. If your users read in an F, they are missing content.
  • The Z-pattern comes from layout practice, not the same eye-tracking evidence. It is a useful convention for sparse pages, moving the eye from logo to headline to call-to-action. Treat it as a first draft, not as research.
  • "F or Z?" is the wrong question. The right one is: what is the single thing this person must understand first?

The question I ask now: what must this screen answer in two seconds?

At NOTES Healthcare ERP I redesigned how doctors read their pay. A doctor has about ten minutes between patients, and the first question is always the same: how much did I earn this month? So profile and income sit on the left, at the top, exactly where the eye lands first. The answer arrives in under two seconds, with no scrolling.

Anchoring key information to reading patterns cut time-to-comprehension by about 40% in testing.

I used the first horizontal sweep of the F deliberately, then broke the F for everything else. Instead of making doctors read down the left edge, the dashboard leads with a summary, and every number is one tap away from its detail.

Turn the F's weakness into a rule: front-load everything

  • Put the point in the first line, not the last. Headings should state the takeaway, not just the topic.
  • Make the left edge carry meaning. In tables, the first column should be what people scan for, like a patient name or an amount.
  • Give every block a heading people can skim. When headings do their job, readers jump from heading to heading and stop where they need to. Nielsen Norman Group calls this the layer-cake pattern, and it is the one you want.

Where the Z-pattern still earns its place: landing pages

Landing pages are sparse and have one goal, which is where Z-pattern thinking helps: logo, headline, proof, then the action. At DSCommerce I designed 29+ landing pages for the DACH market. The pattern gave me a starting layout; A/B testing told me what actually worked. Through that combination of UX optimization and testing, conversion improved by 116%.

Measure the scan path instead of assuming it

A pattern is a hypothesis. Heatmaps and session recordings show you where attention really goes and where it stops. At DSCommerce I used Microsoft Clarity, Google Analytics, heatmaps and session recordings to find friction before changing anything, which is far more reliable than arguing about F or Z in a design review.

The checklist I use in reviews

  • Can someone get the main point from the first line of each section?
  • Is the primary action where the eye ends up, not just where the page starts?
  • Would the screen still work if people only read the left edge?
  • Have I checked real behavior, through a test, heatmap or recording, before calling it done?

In 2023 I asked "F or Z?" Now I ask what a person must understand in two seconds, and how I will know they did.
