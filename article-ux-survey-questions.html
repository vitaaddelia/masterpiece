UX surveys: write fewer questions, get better answers

By Vita Addelia, Product Designer

My 2023 article was about wording: avoid leading questions, keep them short, use clear scales. That advice still holds. What I would add now is the part that matters more: deciding whether a survey is the right tool at all, and making every question earn its place.

Surveys tell you "how many". Interviews tell you "why".

When I redesigned the salary experience at NOTES Healthcare ERP, the problem was that doctors could not understand their own pay. A survey would have told us satisfaction was low. It would not have told us what to build. So we talked to doctors and kept their exact words.

"I just want to open one screen and see: is everything okay this month? Yes or no."

That one sentence became a design principle: show the summary first. Another doctor described emailing HR and waiting three days for an answer, which became our second principle: every number must be traceable. No rating scale would have produced either insight.

Look at behavior before you ask anything

People are poor at predicting their own behavior and good at describing recent experiences. So I start with what they actually do. At DSCommerce I used Microsoft Clarity, Google Analytics, heatmaps and session recordings to find where people struggled. Then questions could target real friction, such as "What stopped you from finishing your order?", instead of generic satisfaction.

Writing the questions (updated from 2023)

  • One idea per question. "Was the checkout fast and clear?" is two questions. Split it.
  • Ask about the past, not the future. "How many times did you do this last month?" beats "Would you use this?"
  • Remove leading words. "How easy was it?" assumes it was easy. Ask "How easy or difficult was it?"
  • Balance your scales, and add "Not applicable" so people are not forced to guess.
  • Put personal questions last, after people have built some trust.
  • Keep it short. Every extra question lowers completion and the quality of later answers.

Map every question to a decision

Before a question goes in, I write down the decision it will inform. If nobody can name one, the question goes. For example, "Which of these did you try before contacting support?" decides which self-serve path to build first. "How satisfied are you overall?" rarely decides anything on its own.

Pilot it like a usability test

Run the survey with three to five people and watch them answer. You will find confusing wording within minutes. At Kampus Indonesia, usability testing and user interviews are what improved onboarding and course discovery. A survey deserves the same rigor, because a bad question produces confident-looking data that is wrong.

Combine methods

At Diceritain, a mental-health peer-support app, qualitative research with students and young professionals came first, and it supported product growth to more than 27,000 user session hours. Qualitative work tells you what to measure; quantitative work tells you how much it matters. Use both.
